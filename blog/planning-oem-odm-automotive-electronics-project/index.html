1. How do you start an automotive electronics OEM/ODM project?

Start with a written product brief, agree on the customization scope, validate a sample and freeze the approved configuration before production. Include the destination market, vehicle applications, must-have functions, target quantity and launch requirements. Confirm engineering costs, ownership, compliance responsibilities and acceptance criteria in writing.

A logo change, a software adaptation and a new hardware design are different projects. Define the actual work and deliverables before comparing quotations labelled “OEM” or “ODM.”

This guide is for distributors, installers and private-label brands planning aftermarket Android head units, CarPlay displays, vehicle cameras, dash cams or related electronics. The stages below are a planning framework; availability and project scope must be confirmed for the selected product.

2. OEM, ODM or private label: which scope fits your project?

Suppliers use these terms differently. OEM usually means original equipment manufacturer, while ODM means original design manufacturer. In aftermarket sourcing, the practical distinction is who provides the base design and who performs each development task. A contract should define those responsibilities instead of relying on the label.

Project routeTypical work to discussWhat to confirm
Private label on an existing productApproved logo, packaging, manual or startup branding.Artwork rules, minimum batch, setup charges and exact configuration.
Adapt an existing supplier platformSelected software, harness, accessories, screen or feature options.Platform limits, development scope, validation and update support.
Build to a buyer specificationBuyer-defined requirements, mechanical design or electronics changes.Design responsibility, engineering fees, tooling, ownership and test evidence.

Choose the smallest scope that meets your customers' requirements. An existing configuration may be sufficient; a new enclosure or board can introduce tooling, component sourcing and additional validation. These routes do not establish a universal price, MOQ or delivery time.

3. Write a product brief that makes quotations comparable

3.1 Define the market and application

List destination countries, sales channel, intended use, vehicle make/model/year and original equipment details. For a head unit, include dashboard photos, factory amplifier, steering-wheel controls and camera requirements. For cameras, identify the receiving display, video mode, supply voltage, connector and installed cable length.

3.2 Separate essential functions from optional features

State measurable requirements for screen, memory, connectivity, audio, recording, interfaces and environmental conditions as applicable. Write a use case and acceptance method for each essential function. “Stable Bluetooth calls in our target vehicle” is more useful when paired with named phones, test conditions and an agreed pass criterion.

3.3 Describe the business constraints

Give sample quantity, first-order quantity, annual forecast, target price basis and launch window. Distinguish a confirmed order from a planning forecast. Specify whether a target cost is ex-works, FOB or landed, including a named location and the intended Incoterms rule where applicable.

Purchasing hardware does not automatically transfer source code, tooling rights or exclusive sales rights. If those matter to your project, define the deliverables, access, territory, duration and conditions before paying development fees.

5. A practical 7-stage project plan

  1. Discovery and feasibility: review the brief, target vehicle applications, existing platform options and unresolved technical questions. Output: a written scope with assumptions and exclusions.
  2. Quotation and project agreement: separate unit cost, engineering, tooling, samples, testing and logistics. Output: agreed deliverables, responsibilities and payment milestones.
  3. Configuration or prototype sample: evaluate the proposed platform and requested changes. Output: a sample with identifiable hardware and firmware revisions.
  4. Validation and corrective work: test the agreed features in the intended system and vehicles. Output: test results, issue list, fixes and documented limitations.
  5. Design/configuration freeze: approve the reference sample, software, accessories, artwork and inspection criteria. Output: a signed production baseline.
  6. Pilot build and production release: where appropriate, use a small production run to check repeatability, assembly, packaging and inspection. Output: release approval or corrective actions.
  7. Shipment and ongoing support: confirm pre-shipment evidence, traceability, documentation, spares and defect procedures. Output: shipment release and a support contact/process.

Simple branding work may combine stages; new hardware may need multiple iterations. Ask for a schedule linked to approvals and component availability. Avoid treating a first sample date as a guaranteed mass-production delivery date.

6. What should you test before production approval?

Agree on the test matrix before sampling, including the method, equipment, conditions, sample quantity and pass criteria. The relevant tests depend on the product and market.

  • Core operation: power-up, repeated power cycles, user interface, audio, recording or camera display as applicable.
  • Vehicle fit and retained functions: installation, harnesses, mounting, steering controls, original amplifier/camera and relevant vehicle variants.
  • Connectivity: specified phones and operating systems, wired/wireless operation, calls, reconnection and supported regional functions.
  • Camera compatibility: input/output format, timing, voltage, pinout, orientation and the actual installed cable. See the AHD vs CVBS compatibility guide.
  • Installed electrical conditions: supply behaviour, grounding, interference and any agreed operating limits, checked using suitable equipment.
  • Environmental and mechanical requirements: temperature, vibration, ingress protection and mounting where relevant, with evidence tied to the exact model and test conditions.
  • Packaging and traceability: labels, serial/batch identifiers, accessories, manuals and shipping protection.

Record issues, ownership, deadlines and retest results. A demonstration video helps explain behaviour, but does not replace the agreed test report or destination-market documentation.

7. How to review MOQ, cost and lead time

There is no single MOQ or timeline for automotive electronics customization. Request separate figures for the standard product, branded batch, customized software and new hardware. Ask which costs repeat with each order and which are one-time charges.

  • Unit price by configuration and order quantity
  • Engineering or non-recurring engineering (NRE), tooling and artwork charges
  • Sample, revision and retest costs, including what is included
  • Market-specific testing, qualification and documentation responsibilities
  • Accessory, packaging, freight and import-cost assumptions
  • Component lead times, buyer approval dates and production capacity assumptions
  • Warranty, spare parts, defect handling and update support

Request a milestone schedule showing dependencies. If a launch date is fixed, identify the latest acceptable dates for scope freeze, sample approval and production release. Agree how a specification change affects cost and delivery before work starts.

8. Plan market access and branding responsibilities early

Identify the destination market and exact product configuration before making certification or compatibility claims. Hardware, radio, firmware and branding changes can affect the evidence needed. Review applicable requirements with the responsible importer, test provider or other qualified specialist.

The EU's product-compliance guidance explains that selling a product under your own name or trademark can place manufacturer responsibilities on your business. Bluetooth SIG also sets qualification and trademark requirements for companies representing Bluetooth products as their own. Review the official references for your scenario; a supplier's existing documentation should not be assumed to cover a newly branded or modified product automatically.

For phone-projection features and third-party applications, confirm supported implementations, permissions, licensing and the exact claims permitted for your product. Keep declarations, reports, labels and technical files aligned with the shipped model. This planning checklist does not establish certification for any Navlumo model.

9. Avoid the project mistakes that create rework

  • Starting artwork before scope is agreed: freeze the product configuration and required labels before final packaging approval.
  • Testing only on a bench: include target vehicles and installed harnesses when integration matters.
  • Approving an unnamed sample: identify hardware, firmware, accessories and artwork revisions.
  • Accepting silent substitutions: agree how component, firmware or supplier changes are reviewed and revalidated.
  • Leaving support undefined: document issue-report requirements, firmware availability, spare parts and defect-handling responsibilities.
  • Assuming “exclusive” means ownership: state the relevant rights, territory, duration and deliverables in the agreement.

11. Frequently asked questions

11.1 What is the difference between OEM and ODM in automotive electronics?

The terms describe different design and manufacturing arrangements, but suppliers use them differently. Confirm who provides the base design, who develops changes, what is delivered and who owns the results. A detailed scope is more reliable than the label alone.

11.2 Can I start with only a logo and packaging change?

That may be possible on an existing platform. Availability, artwork requirements, setup costs and minimum batch size depend on the selected product. Confirm the exact configuration and shipped branding before sampling.

11.3 What is the MOQ for a customized car head unit or camera?

There is no universal MOQ. Standard, private-label, software-customized and new-hardware projects can have different minimums. Request a quotation against your actual scope and quantity.

11.4 How long does an OEM/ODM project take?

Timing depends on the platform, engineering scope, component availability, validation and approval cycles. Request a milestone schedule with assumptions instead of relying on a generic delivery promise.

11.5 Do I need to test a customized sample?

Yes. Confirm the requested changes and the complete intended system against agreed acceptance criteria before production approval. Vehicle integration may require testing on the relevant vehicle variants.

11.6 What information should I send Navlumo first?

Send your product category, destination market, target vehicles, essential functions, requested changes, quantity and launch window. Include photos or specifications for the existing system where relevant.
